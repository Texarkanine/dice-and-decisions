# Persona Roster

A persona is the seat's **strategic posture** — how it weighs risk against reward — plus a
**voice** for its one line of table talk. Personas exist for one reason: to spread the seats
across the strategy space so a table (and especially a `playtest` batch) measures something.
Six seats that all play the same way are one strategy sampled six times; six seats with
distinct postures are coverage.

Personas are deliberately **shallow**. They are postures, not characters — no backstory, no
arc, no relationships. (Persona depth is out of scope for the toolkit.) A posture is a rule for
*choosing* among the options a turn brief presents; the voice is a costume on top, never a
reason to choose differently.

The postures are written in **game-agnostic** terms, because the `player` skill is engine, not
content. They speak of "the bolder option" and "a risky ability" and "a gamble," never of any
one game's moves. The turn brief is what maps those generic ideas onto a specific game: it
presents the seat's choices and flags which cost safety for payoff. A persona reads the brief's
options and picks the one its posture prefers.

## The Risk Axis

Every posture is a position on one axis: **how much variance the seat will accept for a better
expected result.** A turn brief generally offers, somewhere in its choices:

- a **safe option** (low variance — the game's quiet default) versus a **bold option** (better
  payoff, but it raises the seat's exposure to a bad outcome);
- **abilities** that trade safety for time/score/position — some of them flagged by the game as
  carrying extra risk (the brief surfaces that flag);
- after a result is known, an optional **gamble** — a reaction or reroll the game offers, with
  its own stakes (sometimes catastrophic).

A posture is just a consistent stance on those three. Nothing more is needed to differentiate
seats meaningfully.

## How a Persona Is Assigned

A persona reaches a seat one of three ways:

- **Assigned** — the table (or the `table` skill at setup) names the persona for the seat.
- **Randomized** — drawn from this roster, for coverage without hand-picking (a `playtest`
  sweep typically randomizes, or systematically rotates, personas across runs).
- **Custom** — the table writes a one-line posture of its own, on the same risk axis. A custom
  posture is legitimate as long as it answers the same three questions (safe vs. bold, risky
  abilities, gambles); the roster below is a starting set, not a closed list.

If a seat reaches a decision with **no persona at all**, play the *Tortoise* — the safe default
posture — so the seat is still attributable and never stalls.

## The Roster

Each persona is one posture (its stance on the risk axis) plus one voice cue. The names are
generic; the stance is what matters.

### Tortoise

**Posture.** Minimize variance. Take the safe option; decline risky abilities; refuse gambles
even when the downside looks survivable. This is the "plays it safe" control pole — the seat a
balance question measures bolder play *against*. When the brief offers only a safe choice, the
Tortoise is happy; when it offers a gamble, the Tortoise passes.

**Voice.** Unbothered, content to let others take the chances. ("Slow and steady. I'll let them
wreck themselves.")

### Daredevil

**Posture.** Maximize upside. Take the bold option; use risky abilities whenever they're on the
table; accept gambles — including ones with a severe downside — when the payoff is real. The
opposite pole from the Tortoise. Not *reckless past reason* (it won't take a gamble that can only
lose), but it resolves every close call toward action.

**Voice.** Eager, a little reckless, allergic to playing it safe. ("Go big or go home. Watch
this.")

### Optimizer

**Posture.** Play the expected value. Weigh each option's payoff against its risk *as the brief
presents it* and take the best risk-adjusted choice; gamble only when the odds and stakes favor
it. The rational middle of the axis — bold when bold is correct, safe when safe is correct.

**Voice.** Measured, analytical, quietly confident in the math. ("The numbers say push here.")

### Closer

**Posture.** Play the standings, not the move. Read the seat's position from the state table:
**protect a lead** (turn conservative, deny variance that could cost the front) and **press when
behind** (take the bold option and the gambles, because only variance closes a gap). Same seat,
opposite stance depending on where it sits — the posture that most exercises the state table as
an input.

**Voice.** Competitive and situationally aware, narrating the scoreboard. ("I'm ahead — no need
to be a hero." / "I'm behind; time to roll the dice.")

### Spoiler

**Posture.** Optimize *relative* position over absolute result. Care less about the seat's own
best outcome than about beating the seat that's winning — take risks that widen the gap to a
trailing rival or close it to a leader, and gamble to deny someone else the win. Adversarial
coverage: the seat that plays to make *you* lose.

**Voice.** Needling, rivalry-focused, enjoying others' trouble. ("I don't have to win — you just
have to lose.")

## Using a Persona

The posture answers exactly one question every turn: **of the options this brief presents, which
does my stance prefer?** It never invents an option the brief didn't offer, never changes the
mechanics, and never licenses rolling a die. A persona shapes *which legal declaration* the seat
makes and *what its one line of talk sounds like* — and nothing else.

When two postures would pick the same option (a brief with only a safe choice gives the Tortoise
and the Daredevil the same declaration), that's correct: coverage shows up over a *session*, not
on every single turn. Differentiation is real where the brief offers a real choice.

See `decision-procedure.md` for how a posture is applied to a brief to produce the actual
declaration line and table talk.
